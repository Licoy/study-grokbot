## What this issue covers {#intro}

Over the last dozen or so issues, we took GrokBot apart feature by feature: skills, routines, the cloud computer, approvals, memory, multi-Bot collaboration, and more. But many readers tell us that even with all of that, they open a chat and still aren't sure what to ask their Bot to do first.

The official docs have a page just for this, "Use cases", and it lays out a clear path: let a Bot be your assistant, give the tasks you repeat to Bots with one job, then build a team of Bots. This issue turns those three steps into five tips, each with a prompt you can adapt right away.

::: callout The one thing to remember
Connect your apps and let a Bot be your assistant, give each repeated task to a Bot with one job, and give bigger goals to a team of Bots. Every step stops for your review, and sending, publishing, and payments stay behind approval.
:::

## Five tips you can use today {#tips}

Each tip comes with an example prompt. Replace the parts in square brackets with your own details and send it.

### Tip 1: Connect the apps you use, and let a Bot be your assistant {#tip1}

Start by connecting the mail, calendar, chat, and docs you already use. Once they're connected, every Bot you run can read and work in them. For services without an app, a Bot uses the browser on the cloud computer. See [issue 4](/issues/2026-11-04/) for how to connect apps.

The official page gives a few examples of what a Bot can do once your apps are connected:

::: props
- Google Drive: Reads the briefs and past decks in a folder, drafts the next one in your format, and leaves it in Drive for your edits
- Google Calendar: Every Sunday evening, reads the week ahead, flags clashes and packed days, and sends you a short plan for Monday
- Gmail: Sorts the night's mail before you're up, drafts replies to the threads that need you, and leaves every draft for you to send
- Granola: Each evening, reads the day's meeting notes, pulls out every action item with its owner, and drafts a team channel post that goes out once you approve
- Slack: Watches for mentions of your name, starts on the ask, like a piece of copy, and sends you a draft to approve before it replies
- Notion: Every Friday, drafts a progress update for each project page, highlights what changed, and adds it once you approve
:::

You can also just ask a Bot for things you'd otherwise look up yourself, like what changed overnight, who's in your next meeting, or what a competitor shipped this week. It reads across the apps you connected and drafts the next step for you.

::: prompt Example prompt · copy and send to your Bot
Put together my morning briefing for today:
1. Sum up what changed since yesterday in my mail, calendar, [chat app], and docs.
2. List the items that need me personally.
3. Draft it as a post for [team channel]. Don't send it.
:::

### Tip 2: Start from five ready-made assistant use cases {#tip2}

For the assistant stage, the official page offers five use cases, each with a starter prompt. What they have in common: the results come to you first, and the Bot doesn't send, book, or buy anything on your behalf.

| Use case | What you get |
| --- | --- |
| Morning briefing | A morning summary of what needs you, ready to post |
| Inbox manager | Urgent threads first, with replies drafted for your OK |
| Competitive intelligence | What competitors launched or repriced this week, with links |
| Travel coordinator | The two best flight and hotel options, for you to pick |
| Meeting prep | A one-page brief on who's in the room, your last touch, and open threads |

When you adapt the official prompts, the key is to keep the closing line, such as "don't send anything" or "don't book anything".

::: prompt Example prompt · copy and send to your Bot
Prep me for my [2 PM today] meeting:
1. Who is attending, and what did we last discuss?
2. Which threads are still open?
3. Keep it to one page.
Only gather information. Don't message anyone for me.
:::

### Tip 3: Give the request you make every week to a one-job Bot {#tip3}

Watch for the asks you make every week, like qualifying inbound leads or matching invoices. Give each one to a Bot with that single job, so it builds up the context to do it well.

Start new work with your primary Bot (the one marked with a star in the sidebar). When the same kind of request keeps coming back with its own sources, rules, schedule, or approval needs, give it its own Bot, or ask your primary Bot to create one.

The official examples of one-job Bots cover a lot of roles:

| One-job Bot | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Sales development | Qualifies the morning's inbound leads against your ideal customer profile, drafts intros for good fits and a polite no for the rest |
| Invoice coordinator | Matches invoices in the finance inbox to purchase orders, lists mismatches, and drafts a note to each owner |
| Content marketer | Pulls what you shipped this week from the changelog and chat, and drafts the newsletter and posts in your voice |
| Talent scout | Finds candidates who meet the must-haves and drafts outreach in your voice |
| Expense manager | Builds the weekly expense summary, matches receipts, and flags policy exceptions |
| Bug reproduction | Reproduces a bug in staging with a fresh test account, with steps, screenshots, and browser details |
| Account health | Ranks accounts by churn risk and expansion potential from usage, tickets, and renewal dates |

All of these stop at drafts and recommendations: they don't contact anyone, approve payments, change budgets, or touch production.

::: prompt Example prompt · copy and send to your Bot
I want to create a Bot with one job. Help me plan it:
1. It only handles [one task, like matching expense receipts every week].
2. Which apps it needs and which sources it reads.
3. Which actions must ask me first, like sending, paying, and deleting.
Show me the plan first, and create it only after I confirm.
:::

### Tip 4: Get it working once, then let the one-job Bot run on its own {#tip4}

When a one-job Bot does the work the way you want, you can let it run on its own. The official page suggests three steps:

::: steps
- State the job and have it remembered | Tell the Bot its job and its limits in chat, and ask it to remember them. Rules that last go to memory; the details of a single task go in your message.
- Save a skill, then create a routine | Save the working steps as a skill, then create a routine to run it on a schedule. A routine can also start when something happens, like a chat message or an email to your Bot.
- Keep logins on a secure path | Save test logins in Vault or enter them in a Secure Form, never in chat.
:::

One more thing that's easy to miss: for numbers that change, like prices or budgets, ask the Bot to look them up each time instead of relying on what it remembers.

::: prompt Example prompt · copy and send to your Bot
That [expense summary] came out well. Let's make it a standing job:
1. Turn your job and limits into a few rules, and remember them after I confirm.
2. Save the steps you just took as a skill.
3. Create a routine that runs [every Friday afternoon] and sends me the result to review.
Look up every amount fresh each time. Don't reuse old numbers from memory.
:::

### Tip 5: Give bigger goals to a team of Bots {#tip5}

Some goals take more than one job, like a launch campaign or the month-end close. Ask your primary Bot to set up a few Bots that each own one part. They pass the work along, and you check the result. If you're not sure how to split the work, start from [dr eggbot](https://x.ai/bot/marketplace/bots/dr-eggbot-v2) in the official marketplace: it asks you a few questions, then sets up the Bots for you, one job each.

The official page gives three team examples:

| Team | Who does what | When it runs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Campaign team | Pulls last week's sign-ups by channel, drafts a launch note and three posts from the numbers, then checks the drafts against the numbers and brand doc | Mondays at 9 AM |
| Month-end close | Matches invoices to purchase orders, reconciles expense reports with receipts, and lists every mismatch for your sign-off | The 1st of each month |
| Support queue | Sorts new tickets and drafts replies, reproduces bug reports in staging, and flags help pages that need fixing | Weekdays at 9 AM |

When Bots work together in a group chat, make one Bot responsible for each step so ownership stays clear. See [issue 7](/issues/2026-11-07/) for more on multi-Bot collaboration and handoffs.

::: prompt Example prompt · copy and send to your Bot
I want a team of Bots for [a goal, like sorting reader mail every month]:
1. Split it into three or four steps, with one Bot responsible for each.
2. For each Bot, say what it reads, what it produces, and who it hands off to.
3. Suggest when it should run and which steps must wait for my approval.
Show me the plan first. Don't create any Bots yet.
:::

## Limits you should know {#limits}

::: steps
- Every step stops for you | All the official examples stop for your review. Keep sending, publishing, purchasing, and production changes behind approval.
- Back it up with Ask first | Under **Custom Rules** in **Settings → General → Bot**, add **Ask first** rules for sending, publishing, and deleting. To approve every payment yourself, don't save the card in Vault.
- The computer is shared | All your Bots share one cloud computer. Its files and browser sign-ins are available to your other Bots too.
- Keep secrets out of chat | Passwords go through Vault, 1Password, or a Secure Form. Cards go through Vault or a Secure Form. API keys go through a secure secret card.
- A Team Bot can't be your primary Bot | Your primary Bot is your main point of contact. It checks in with you on its own and coordinates your other Bots. A Team Bot can't take that role.
:::

## Case study: a fictional handmade shop, from one assistant to a small team {#case}

A fictional scenario (not anyone's real setup): the owner of a small handmade goods shop started by asking the primary Bot to sort customer email every morning. A few weeks in, the owner noticed the same requests coming up every week and handed the work over one step at a time.

| Stage | What they did | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assistant | The primary Bot sorted customer email each morning into urgent, needs a reply, and FYI, and drafted replies | Drafts only; the owner sent replies personally |
| One-job Bot | Created "Returns Helper" to handle only return and exchange emails | Asked it to remember the return policy and leave other mail alone |
| Running on its own | Saved the steps as a skill and set a routine to run every morning | Added an Ask first rule for refunds |
| Team | Added "Stock Helper" and "Weekly Report Helper", coordinated by the primary Bot | One Bot responsible for each step in the group chat |

### Highlight: handing over a little at a time keeps mistakes easy to fix {#case-flow}

The owner didn't start with a team. Each Bot proved it could do its own job first, and only then were they connected.

::: flow
- Me | Explains what to hand over this week | person
- Primary Bot | Splits the work and assigns it
- Returns Helper | Drafts replies to return requests
- Me | Reviews and approves refunds | person
---
- Me → Primary Bot: I don't want to handle return emails anymore. Set up a Bot just for that
- Primary Bot → Me: I've drafted Returns Helper's job and rules. I'll create it once you confirm
- Returns Helper → Me: Three return emails today. Replies are drafted, and one refund is waiting for your approval
- Me → Returns Helper: The first two are fine. Hold the refund until I've checked with the customer
:::

Later, the owner asked Weekly Report Helper to sum up the week's returns and stock every Sunday evening, so there's just one page to read.

::: prompt Example prompt · copy and send to your Bot
You are this "Returns Helper". From now on, follow these rules:
1. Only handle return and exchange emails. Pass everything else to the primary Bot.
2. Draft replies but don't send them, and wait for my approval on every refund.
3. After each morning's run, tell me how many emails came in and which ones need me.
Restate the rules first.
:::

## Community picks: common ways to use it {#community}

This issue collects a few common, easy-to-start ideas. They're general patterns; swap in your own details.

### End prompts with "don't send" {#c1}

Finish a task with "draft only, don't send" or "don't book anything". Look at the result first, then decide whether to let go.

### One task, one Bot {#c2}

When you catch yourself repeating the same request every week, hand it to a one-job Bot so it builds up memory for that job.

### Get it right once, then save a skill {#c3}

Work through the task with your Bot once until it's right, then save a skill and create a routine. That's more reliable than writing a long list of rules up front.

### No app? Ask your Bot to add one {#c4}

If a service you use often has no app, ask a Bot to connect its MCP server. For a service that only has an API, try [tinkabot](https://x.ai/bot/marketplace/bots/tinkabot) in the official marketplace. It tests what it builds and publishes only when you say so.

### Draw the org chart before building a team {#c5}

Before you build a team of Bots, ask your primary Bot for a table: what each Bot reads, what it produces, and who it hands off to. Create the Bots after you confirm.
