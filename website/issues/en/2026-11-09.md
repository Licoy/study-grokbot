## What this issue covers {#intro}

Earlier issues were about you and your own Bots. This one asks a different question: once you've tuned a Bot, how do you hand it to someone else? The docs describe two very different ways to share. The names sound alike but the effects are not, and mixing them up is where things go wrong.

A **template** is a link that gives the other person their own copy. A **shared Bot** is the Bot itself, shared with your Cursor team, so everyone chats with the same one. Pick well and sharing stays safe and simple. Pick wrong and either people can't change it, or you hand over things you didn't mean to.

::: callout The one thing to remember
Send a template when everyone should get their own; share the Bot when everyone should use the same one. Audit what's inside before you share, because whatever you share works in every chat.
:::

## Five tips you can use today {#tips}

Each tip comes with an example prompt. Replace the parts in brackets with your own and send it to your Bot.

### Tip 1: Tell a template from a shared Bot {#tip1}

Start from the result you want, then choose.

A template fits when:

::: props
- Everyone gets their own: Each person gets a copy and changes it independently
- How it's sent: One link; the recipient previews it on x.ai and adds it to Grok Bot
- What they don't get: Your computer, logins, or conversation history
:::

A shared Bot fits when:

::: props
- Everyone uses one: Teammates chat with the same Bot; sharing doesn't make a copy
- Who it's for: Members of a Cursor team who have Grok Bot
- Privacy: Each person's chat is private, even from the owner and Managers
:::

A shared Bot's link opens the same Bot for everyone, which is different from a template that gives each person a copy. Shared Bots always run in the cloud, so a Bot that runs on your own computer can't be shared.

::: prompt Example prompt · copy and send to your Bot
Help me decide whether this Bot should be shared as a template or shared with my team:
What the Bot does: [one sentence]
Who will use it: [teammates / friends or colleagues / the public]
Do they need to change it themselves: [yes/no]
Are we all on the same Cursor team: [yes/no]
Does this Bot run on my own computer: [yes/no]
Give a three-sentence recommendation with reasons. Don't create a template or change any sharing settings yet.
:::

### Tip 2: Send a template: Share, Create a copy, Publish {#tip2}

The documented steps are short:

::: steps
- Open details | Click the Bot's name at the top of the chat.
- Create a copy | Choose Share, then Create a copy. The Bot builds a template and posts it in the chat.
- Publish and copy | On the template card, choose Publish, then Copy link, and send the link.
- Recipient adds it | They open a preview on x.ai and choose Add to Grok Bot. They need the Grok Bot app to finish adding it.
:::

On iPhone and Android, open the Bot's menu and choose Share as Template. After you publish, the same Share menu offers View template details, Update template (push your latest changes to the shared copy), and Delete Template (turn off the link).

A new template link is public. If your team allows only team-only sharing or has turned template sharing off, the app doesn't offer Create a copy.

::: prompt Example prompt · copy and send to your Bot
I want to share this Bot as a template. Do a check only; don't publish anything:
1. List what in its identity, description, skills, and routines would go out with a template;
2. Flag anything that may contain API keys, internal URLs, customer data, or anything not meant to be public;
3. Give me a list of what I should change first.
Once I confirm the cleanup, I'll ask you to continue with Create a copy and Publish.
:::

### Tip 3: Before you publish, clean up as if it were a public document {#tip3}

A template link exposes the Bot's shared configuration: identity, description, skills, and routines. The docs' advice is to remove API keys, internal URLs, customer data, and anything you wouldn't put in a public document.

::: props
- Goes out: Identity, description, skills, routines
- Doesn't go out: Your computer, logins, conversation history
- Public link: Anyone who has it can open it
- Team-only link: Opens only for members of your team
:::

The security docs also say sharing a Bot is not a security boundary. A public link lets others copy the configuration; it doesn't protect what's inside. Don't write a key into a skill or description because "they can't get my logins anyway."

Also, adding someone else's template shows the third-party bot terms. Templates are created by other users, not by Grok Bot, so review one the way you'd review a skill or software.

::: prompt Example prompt · copy and send to your Bot
Review what this Bot would share, using the standard "would I put this in a public document":
1. Check identity, description, skills, and routines one by one;
2. List the location of every key, internal URL, customer record, or personal detail, and suggest a placeholder;
3. Secrets should live in Vault or a secure card, not in these places;
4. Give me a before/after summary to confirm.
Don't change anything and don't publish until I confirm.
:::

### Tip 4: Shared Bots: who can use, whose apps, who pays {#tip4}

To share a Bot, click its name at the top of the chat and choose Share. Under Who has access, pick your team for everyone, or People you invite for specific people and groups. Use Add people or groups, and under Add as choose:

::: props
- Collaborator: Can chat with the Bot
- Manager: Can also change its instructions, skills, memory, and apps
- Owner and Managers: Can always use the Bot
:::

Rules worth remembering:

::: steps
- Apps and secrets | A shared Bot uses the apps and secrets its owner added, the same for everyone. Only add what you're happy for everyone to use.
- Your personal accounts | In your own chat it can also use your apps, but asks first: Allow once, Always allow, or Skip.
- Usage | A reply uses the Grok Bot usage of the person it's answering, and your own limits apply.
- Approvals | It asks the person it's answering before risky actions; in a group chat only that person can answer.
- How teammates find it | Choose + then Browse shared Bots and choose Add, or open a link a teammate sent and choose Start a chat.
:::

Before you share, check the Bot's apps, secrets, skills, and files, since they work in every teammate's chat.

::: prompt Example prompt · copy and send to your Bot
I'm about to share this Bot with my team. Audit only; don't change any sharing settings:
1. List the apps, saved secrets, skills, and files it has now;
2. For each, say whether I should keep it given that everyone I share with can use it;
3. Suggest who should be a Collaborator and who a Manager (use [role name] placeholders);
4. Remind me that each reply uses the usage of the person it answers.
Give me a keep/remove table and wait for my confirmation.
:::

### Tip 5: Bring a shared Bot into Slack {#tip5}

After sharing, the owner or a Manager can connect it to Slack:

::: steps
- Open details | Click the Bot's name at the top of the chat.
- Connect Slack | Find Bring to your team's Slack and choose Connect.
- Admin approval | If it says Awaiting admin approval, a Slack workspace admin needs to approve the app.
- Teammates link accounts | Each teammate first opens Settings > General > Shared Bots and chooses Link for their Slack account.
- Start using it | Once it says Connected, teammates can message it directly or mention it in channels.
:::

In Slack channels, threads, and group chats, a shared Bot uses only its owner's apps and asks before using your accounts; in channels, threads, group DMs, and group chats it works on one shared computer of its own. In a thread, Bots record who asked for each message, so anyone can ask who requested it. The Review an action card offers Allow once, Deny, and View details, and only the person who asked can answer.

::: prompt Example prompt · copy and send to your Bot
We plan to bring this shared Bot into our team's Slack. Prepare a pre-launch checklist; don't run any connection steps yet:
1. The steps I (owner or Manager) need to take;
2. How to explain the request to a Slack admin if approval is needed;
3. A short note telling each teammate to link their Slack account first;
4. A reminder that in channels it uses only the owner's apps and asks before using personal accounts.
Checklist and wording only; wait for my confirmation.
:::

## Limits you should know {#limits}

::: steps
- Shared Bots are cloud-only | A Bot on your own computer can't be shared, and a shared Bot can't join a group with Bots that run on your own computer.
- Template links start public | Unless your team limits sharing to the team, anyone with a new link can open it.
- Sharing isn't a security boundary | A public link doesn't protect its contents; keep keys, customer data, and internal URLs out.
- Shared things work for everyone | Apps, secrets, skills, and files the owner adds work in every teammate's chat.
- Deleting affects the whole team | Only the owner can delete a shared Bot; teammates lose it along with their chats and routines on it, and it can't be undone.
- A shared Bot can't be primary | On the phone you can chat with it and answer Allow cards, but it can't be your primary Bot.
- Your team may limit templates | Admins can turn off public templates, in which case the app doesn't offer Create a copy.
:::

## Case study: a fictional weekly-report squad shares a Bot {#case}

Continuing the fictional lineup from earlier issues (not anyone's real setup): one operations teammate has tuned a Weekly Report Bot. She wants her team to use it, and also wants to let an outside friend try it.

| Audience | Method | Why | Do before sharing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Teammates | Shared Bot | Everyone uses the same one; apps and skills stay in one place | Audit apps, secrets, files; name one Manager |
| Outside friend | Template | Not on the team; needs their own copy | Strip internal URLs and data from description and skills |
| Team Slack channel | Shared Bot plus Slack | Mention it right in the channel | Admin approval; each person links their account |
| Content not for sharing | Stay in a private Bot | Personal accounts and private data | Keep out of shared Bots and templates |

### Highlight: audit first, pick the method, then release step by step {#case-flow}

She didn't send a link right away. She had the Bot audit its contents, chose a method per audience, and confirmed each step.

::: flow
- Me | Asks the Bot to audit what would be shared | person
- Weekly Report Bot | Lists apps, secrets, skills, files, and routines
- Weekly Report Bot | Flags what can't be public or shared
- Me | Confirms cleanup and picks template or shared
- Weekly Report Bot | Builds the template or adjusts sharing as chosen
---
- Me → Weekly Report Bot: audit first, don't publish
- Weekly Report Bot → Me: list and risk flags
- Me → Weekly Report Bot: confirms cleanup items
- Weekly Report Bot → Me: asks whether to Publish / Share
- Me → Weekly Report Bot: approves, then releases
:::

For the outside friend she used a template with internal details removed; for teammates she shared the same Bot and named a Manager. She never mixed the two.

::: prompt Example prompt · copy and send to your Bot
You're this Weekly Report Bot. Handle the sharing request in this order and stop for my confirmation at each step:
1. Audit apps, secrets, skills, files, and routines;
2. Flag anything that can't be public or shouldn't be shared;
3. Propose sharing for teammates and a template for the outside friend;
4. Until I confirm, don't Publish, don't change sharing, and don't send any link.
Audit and proposal only.
:::

## Community picks: common ways to share {#community}

Here are a few common, easy-to-start ideas. They're general, so swap in your own details.

### Share a clean version {#c1}

Make a version with only general instructions and general skills, send that as the template, and keep private material in your own Bot.

### Keep a cleanup checklist {#c2}

Save "check identity, description, skills, routines one by one" as a snippet and have the Bot run it before every share.

### Name a Manager first {#c3}

Before sharing with a team, decide who can change instructions, skills, and apps, so not everyone edits the same setup.

### Templates outside, shared inside {#c4}

Send templates to people outside your team and share one Bot with people inside it. Keeping the two apart avoids confusion.

### Tell people after you update {#c5}

After pushing changes with Update template, tell the recipients; the docs don't say whether copies already added change.
